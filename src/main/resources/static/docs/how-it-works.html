How the scheduler works

You don't need this page to use the scheduler. It's here if you're curious, or need to explain the results to colleagues.

From sections to meetings

Each section becomes one or more weekly meetings, depending on its duration and its split setting. A 180-minute, 3-unit section with split=yes becomes a 120-minute meeting and a 60-minute meeting. The scheduler's job is to give every meeting a day, a start time and a room.

Your week becomes a grid

Your school day, Monday to Friday, is divided into equal slots sized to fit your meetings. The daily break is left out. A 120-minute meeting needs two 60-minute slots in a row on the same day, without the break in the middle.

The rules

A timetable is feasible when none of these are broken:

  • A lecturer has at most one meeting at a time.
  • A student has at most one meeting at a time. Two sections that share any students can never run at the same time.
  • A room holds at most one meeting at a time.
  • Each meeting is in a room with enough seats, and within any rooms and timeslot restrictions you set.

The search

There are far too many possible timetables to try them all, so the scheduler searches smartly in rounds:

  1. Build. A group of independent builders each assemble a full timetable, placing one meeting at a time and preferring spots that cause the fewest clashes.
  2. Repair. Each timetable is then improved by repeatedly picking a clashing meeting and moving it to the spot with the fewest clashes.
  3. Learn. Placements that appeared in the best timetables are remembered and become more likely in the next round. Placements that didn't help gradually fade.

This is an ant colony method, so called because it works like ants finding the shortest path to food: good routes are reinforced, and poor ones fade.

The search stops as soon as it finds a timetable with no clashes, or when its time limit runs out. In that case it returns the best timetable it found, marked feasible: false.

Why results can differ between runs

Each run makes random choices, so two runs on the same data can give two different, equally valid timetables. If a run ends with clashes, another run may find a clean one.

When more time won't help

Some meetings can't be placed anywhere, for example a section with more students than your largest room. The scheduler spots these before searching and reports them separately, with the reason, so you can fix the data instead of waiting.
